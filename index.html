I’m a Master’s student in Computational Linguistics at the University of Washington in Seattle. My research interests lie at the intersection of linguistics and computational methods, with a particular focus on how humans and language models represent and interpret meaning.

I’m especially interested in combining theoretical linguistic analysis with computational and experimental methods to study semantic representation, ambiguity, and language model behavior. My current work examines how models resolve meaning in context and how linguistic structure can help us better understand their successes and systematic failures.

I previously worked at Korea Electronics Technology Institute (KETI), NCSOFT, and SK Telecom. These experiences shaped my interest in studying language models not only as computational systems, but also as objects of linguistic analysis whose behavior can reveal both the capabilities and limitations of current language technologies.

I hold a B.A. in Linguistics & Cognitive Science and a double major in Language Science in Artificial Intelligence from Hankuk University of Foreign Studies, where I was advised by Prof. Jeesun Nam.

[1] Relational Grounding Failures in Zero-Shot Cross-Lingual Dialogue Summarization with Small Language Models
Accepted at GroundLM 2026, an EMNLP 2026 Workshop | Aug 2026

We investigate how small language models preserve dialogue meaning in zero-shot English-to-Chinese summarization. Using a Dialogue-Semantic Grounding Error Taxonomy, we show that summaries can retain relevant content while distorting speaker roles and other semantic relations, revealing faithfulness failures that reference-based metrics may obscure.

  • Evaluated Aya Expanse 8B, Gemma 4 E4B, and Qwen 3.5 9B on a fixed 100-dialogue XSAMSum test sample, alongside a supervised mBART baseline.
  • Developed a nine-label taxonomy covering six hallucination types, omissions, language or format failures, and error-free outputs.
  • Speaker-role hallucination was the most frequent hallucination type across the combined SLM outputs. mBART achieved the highest ROUGE and BERTScore despite having the lowest error-free rate under the taxonomy.

[1] Probing Lexical Ambiguity Resolution in Language Models through Fine-Grained Semantic Relation Prediction
M.S. Thesis, University of Washington | Mar. 2026–Present

My M.S. thesis investigates how language models resolve lexical ambiguity in context, using fine-grained semantic relation prediction to assess whether models track meaning beyond simple sense selection. I am developing a controlled benchmark of ambiguous nouns and verbs to examine how models relate contextually intended meanings to alternative candidate meanings.

  • Constructing benchmark items that pair an ambiguous word in context with its intended meaning, a candidate meaning, and a semantic relation label.
  • Adapting relations inspired by Natural Logic for nouns and verbs: equivalence, forward entailment, reverse entailment, alternation, and independence, with opposition included for verbs.
  • Designing two prompt-based evaluations: relation selection from a predefined inventory, and structured generation of intended meanings, relation labels, and explanations.
  • The generation outputs will be examined for semantic hallucinations, including plausible but contextually unsupported meanings, relation labels, and explanations.
Lexical Ambiguity Semantic Relations Semantic Diagnostics LM Evaluation
